I studied B.Tech in Smart Manufacturing at IIITDM Chennai — a degree that sits right at the intersection of machines and computation. I came in wired for hardware: circuit boards, mechanisms, the tactile satisfaction of something physical working.

Then somewhere along the way, software pulled me in — the elegance of systems that adapt, the leverage of code that scales. That drift led me straight to robotics — the one discipline where hardware and software aren't just connected, they're inseparable.

Along the way I've met some of the finest minds in Indian robotics, which only sharpened my sense of what's worth building.

For a long time I was focused on finding a real-world problem to solve with robotics. Recently my perspective widened: I'm not just looking at problems the world has that robots could fix — I'm also paying close attention to the unsolved problems inside robotics itself. The field has deep open questions, and those might be worth my time too.

turning point

Hardware gives you the problem. Software gives you the leverage. I wanted both — which is exactly what robotics is.

discipline

The only engineering field where mechanical, electrical, and software constraints are all first-class. You can't afford to ignore any of them.

community

The Indian robotics ecosystem is smaller than it looks from outside, but the people in it are building seriously ambitious things. Meeting them changed what I thought was possible.

old lens

The original framing: find a messy real-world domain — logistics, agriculture, healthcare — and ask "could a robot help here?" Still a valid question.

new lens

The wider framing: robotics itself has unsolved problems — sim-to-real transfer, safe exploration, uncertainty-aware control. Solving these unlocks everything downstream.
